Bullet-Fuselage Interceptor Quadcopter

06/2026 – Present
SolidWorks FEA Aerodynamics
[ Image — CAD render of fuselage / wing / arm layout ]
Overview
Objective

Design a compact, 3D-printed VTOL aircraft that flies as a multirotor for takeoff and landing and transitions to efficient winged forward flight, using off-the-shelf FPV components in a custom modular chassis — inspired by Ukrainian interceptor drone designs.

Constraints
  • Fuselage: ~3" diameter cylindrical shell, ~12" overall length
  • Manufacturing: every part must print on UT Makerspace's Bambu printers (~256×256mm bed) within their 5-hour print-time limit — this drove the modularity of the design
  • Size: 5" prop class drone, 1960KV motors, 6S LiPo battery
Key decisions

Added NACA 2412 wings so the aircraft generates real aerodynamic lift instead of relying on thrust alone. Simplified the arms to flat, symmetric airfoils once it was clear they weren't contributing meaningful lift at their angle to the airflow — not worth the added drag. Chose a tractor motor layout over pusher for cleaner prop-exit airflow.

Iteration

Iterated on aerodynamics and modular-component assembly while reducing weight. Arm geometry and tail layout each went through multiple dead ends before converging — see Failures & Solutions.

Status / result

CAD is finalized around the bullet fuselage, NACA 2412 wing, and flat-arm layout. FEA on the motor arms confirms they're strong enough to support the high-thrust motors without breaking. Propulsion and avionics (stack, motors, props, battery, charger) are selected. Next milestone: first print and build.

Retrospective

The print-bed and time constraints at the makerspace ended up shaping the airframe almost as much as the aerodynamics did — turning a smooth bullet fuselage into fabricatable parts became its own design problem.

Failures and solutions
  • Arm geometry: Initially airfoiled for aerodynamic efficiency, but motor wiring couldn't route through the arm in the tractor configuration. Thickened the arms to route wiring internally, then swept the leading edge into a trapezoidal shape for added strength and lift — before realizing the arms sat at roughly 45° to the airflow and weren't worth using as lifting surfaces. Final solution: flat airfoiled arms for aerodynamic efficiency, with a gap between the motor housing and trailing edge to route wiring without reintroducing the original clearance problem.
  • Tail fin / antenna conflict: Mounting the antenna on the tail created a ground-clearance problem on vertical landing. Solved by sweeping the tail fins back and adding small landing legs at their ends.
  • Wing geometry: Wings were first designed as stabilizers rather than lift-generating surfaces, then switched to a cambered airfoil to extend flight time — which pushed the center of pressure too far forward. Solved by sweeping the wings back to keep the center of gravity ahead of the center of pressure.
  • Early assembly conflicts: Initial section designs were built around wiring simplicity without checking real assembly clearance, and some components couldn't physically connect once modeled together. Reworked the section interfaces around real fit rather than idealized geometry.

Odor-Reducing Laundry Hamper

10/2025 – Present
Onshape Laser Cutting Airflow Design
[ Image — hamper prototype photo ]
Overview
Objective

Design a laundry hamper that actively reduces odor buildup in stored clothing through airflow management, rather than passive storage.

Constraints
  • Manufacturing: laser-cut wood, built at UT's makerspace, limited to the laser cutter's 24"×48" bed
  • Capacity: must hold multiple days' worth of clothing
  • Final prototype footprint: 24"×24"×24"
Key decisions
  • Airflow path pulls air in from the bottom and exhausts out the top to work with natural convection, with the fan mounted at the base.
  • Clothing is stored across multiple drawer-like levels, each with a waffle-pattern hole layout resting between two rail supports — maximizes airflow contact with clothing versus a solid shelf, while keeping the structure light.
Iteration

Originally included carbon filters on the exhaust air for extra odor control; cut them once testing showed the exhaust air wasn't actually smelly, simplifying to fan-only airflow without losing performance. Reworked the box edges into interlocking joints for easier, more repeatable assembly. Added a variable fan-speed controller so airflow velocity could become a testable parameter for drying speed and odor reduction.

Status / result

First prototype built and under active testing. Early results show treated clothing smells noticeably better than untreated control clothing after storage. Next: build a full-size version and validate with real users using blind smell-comparisons against control clothing.

Retrospective

The clearest lesson so far has been to keep the design solution as simple as possible — realizing advanced odor management like carbon filtration wasn't necessary kept the design simpler and cheaper without sacrificing results.

Beetleweight Combat Robot

01/2026 – 05/2026
FEA 3D Printing SPARC Competition
[ Image — robot build or competition photo ]
Overview
Objective

Design and fabricate a beetleweight (≤3 lb) combat robot under SPARC competition rules, prioritizing simplicity, mobility, and offensive capability within a strict budget.

Constraints
  • Weight: 3 lb max (SPARC beetleweight class)
  • Budget: $50 cap on new components — forced reuse of stock parts wherever possible
  • Weapons restricted to kinetic systems only
  • Starting platform: Rhybor AWD RC car, with motors, battery, and receiver identified as reuse candidates
  • Built at UT's makerspace, limited to 3D printing and laser cutting for fabrication
Key decisions
  • Used a two-motor tank drive over the stock AWD layout for zero-radius turning, trading four-wheel-drive traction for a more maneuverable two-wheel setup.
  • Designed the chassis to be fully invertible — mirrored upper/lower panels with wheels extending to both faces so the robot stays drivable if flipped.
  • Split materials by function: PETG for the chassis (impact resistance), PLA for armor and sliders (lower friction).
  • Connected the weapon motor to the spinner through a belt rather than a rigid drivetrain, letting the belt slip under sudden impact to protect the motor and gear train — at the cost of some weapon speed recovery time after a hit.
Iteration

Reworked the slider profile from flat to semicircular, cutting friction and letting the robot roll over arena floor irregularities instead of snagging. Swapped the weapon dowels from wood to metal to reduce friction.

Status / result

The robot was successfully fabricated. The first match ended early due to an axle failure. After addressing this, the robot won a later match in the competition.

Retrospective

Static FEA on 3D-printed parts is only as good as the material model behind it — it can't capture how print orientation changes strength along different axes, and that gap is exactly what took the robot out of its first match despite a clean analysis. With more time, the next version would use a lower-KV weapon motor built for torque instead of RPM, choose print orientation based on actual load direction rather than default settings, and consider a simpler direct-drive weapon system to remove a failure point.

Failures and solutions
  • Weapon motor mismatch: The brushless motor selected was originally built for RC aircraft propellers — high KV (high RPM, low torque) — and couldn't produce enough torque to spin up the aluminum spinner's full mass. Solved by cutting material out of the spinner to reduce rotational inertia, at the cost of some weapon effectiveness.
  • Pulley failure and belt redesign: The spinner was originally driven through a 15-tooth pulley for a set gear ratio, but the motor couldn't sustain the required torque. Removed the pulley and ran the timing belt directly off the motor shaft, letting the motor accelerate the belt through friction alone.
  • Axle failure in competition: Static FEA predicted the 3D-printed axles would survive normal driving loads with a wide margin (FOS of 37), but one axle broke almost immediately in the first match. Likely cause: the axles were printed with layers parallel to the shear plane — the weakest orientation for a 3D-printed part under shear — a failure mode a standard static FEA with an idealized isotropic material model doesn't capture.
